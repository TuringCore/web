Essay

Hardware synthesis

In the beginning, hardware design was not a science of proof but a craft of correlation. A designer drew a logic diagram, built a gate netlist, and hoped the device would behave as intended when the signals began to move at speed. The rituals were elaborate, the tools imperfect, and the language of trust was mostly practical: the piece worked in the lab, the timing looked stable, the board did not smoke under load.

The long arc

What synthesis changed

The great invention of synthesis was not simply automation. It was the idea that logic could be described at a higher level and then mechanically translated into a lower-level implementation. Boolean equations became a design language, then a netlist, then a physical plan. That change mattered because it allowed complexity to scale without requiring every designer to keep every gate in mind at once.

A discipline of obligation

Why the old methods always felt slightly precarious

Before formal tools entered the picture, hardware design often depended on a mixture of simulation, inspection, and instinct. The trouble is that simulation is a sampling method, not a guarantee. It tells you that certain scenarios behaved as expected, not that the architecture is sound for all relevant inputs, timings, and edge conditions. The unspoken anxiety beneath every generation of chip design was this: what if the missing case is the one that matters?

The middle of the story

Formal verification enters the room

Formal methods changed the shape of the conversation. Instead of asking whether a design appears correct under the test vectors one has run, one asks whether it satisfies a model of correctness under a specification. The result is not an opinion but a proof obligation. That shift in posture matters. It moves design from "looks right" to "is constrained in the right way," and from "we believe the logic is safe" to "we can state the conditions under which it is safe."

The system under discussion

Some of the goals of the DPU

The work in /dpu is a good example of this philosophy in miniature. The focus is narrow but meaningful: a tile-level digital processing unit for diffusion-style multiply-accumulate workloads. The design is intentionally small enough to reason about directly, yet rich enough to carry the architecture's central concerns: signed arithmetic, bounded accumulation, valid/ready handshakes, saturation, and output stability under backpressure. These are not decorative details. They are the kinds of obligations that become dangerous once they are scaled into arrays or integrated into processors that never stop moving.

1. A tractable core

The DPU tile is a small, verification-friendly primitive rather than a speculative full accelerator. This makes the specification and proofs easier to hold in view.

2. A clear protocol

The valid/ready interfaces establish the operating discipline explicitly: configuration, data intake, and final output are all part of the machine's contract.

3. Safety under edge cases

Zero-length jobs, saturation, and held-output behavior are not side stories. They are the places where many arithmetic bugs hide in plain sight.

4. A roadmap to scale

The tile is a candidate building block for larger arrays, but the truth of the design is established before the architecture broadens.

The point of the exercise

Not just to build faster hardware, but to build trusted hardware

Formal verification does not remove the need for engineering judgment. It gives judgment a sharper object. One can still choose a saturation policy, a data format, or a throughput target. But once those choices are encoded in a proof-friendly specification, they stop being assumptions floating in the room. They become parts of the system's contract. This is the real value of the modern hardware synthesis stack: not merely that it turns invention into automation, but that it turns design intent into something reviewable, checkable, and reusable.

Outcomes

Three kinds of confidence

Simulation

Behavior is exercised under many concrete trajectories, including randomized and edge-heavy cases.

Formal assertions

Key invariants are stated as obligations that the logic must satisfy in all relevant states.

Reusable proof base

The evidence is retained as a basis for later design revisions rather than discarded after a single pass.
